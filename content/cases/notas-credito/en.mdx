## Context

A credit note corrects an invoice that was already issued: a return, a product that wasn't delivered, a discount. In Mexico it's an outgoing tax document stamped with the tax authority (SAT), and nearly all of its data (payment form, relation type, taxes, currency) depends on the invoice it corrects.

A mistake in that data can't be fixed later. Once stamped, the note is final: it can't be edited or cancelled from the system.

## Who it's for

The team that handles collections for prepaid customers. Each person sees only the customers in their portfolio, browses their notes by customer and issues new ones from there.

## Twelve scenarios, one flow

The tax scenarios look like twelve different processes: physical return, undelivered product, data entry error, negotiated or post-payment discount, partially paid invoice, invoice in dollars, invoice at zero VAT, money refunded to the customer. I designed just one.

The wizard has three steps: find the invoice, enter the data, confirm. From one scenario to another, the data reaching the screen changes, not the screen. That made it possible to document all twelve without multiplying the design.

## Decisions and their cost

### The system handles the tax side

**The problem.** Payment method, payment form and relation type come from tax rules. Asking people for them means asking them to know the rules, and to get it wrong when they don't.

**The decision.** People choose the reason for the note; the system resolves the rest and shows it as data, not as a field. Currency, exchange rate and taxes are inherited from the original invoice: if it was issued at zero VAT, so is the note.

**What it costs.** Someone who knows a rule well can't adjust it by hand. An exception means changing the rule in the system, not the entry.

### The excess, the only extra decision

**The problem.** When the note is larger than what the customer still owes, there's money left over, and someone has to decide what happens to it.

**The decision.** The question only appears when there's an excess. Kept as credit, the note is born with a balance for the next purchase; refunded, the person records how the money was returned. Continuing is blocked until there's an answer.

**What it costs.** The screen changes depending on the invoice data. In exchange, the decision shows up exactly where it's needed, and not before.

### Line items, not amounts

**The problem.** Typing an amount by hand is the easiest way to get a note that corrects specific products wrong.

**The decision.** The invoice's line items are listed with their original quantity and price. You check the item, then enter the quantity; the price can't be edited. If the issue was the price and not the quantity, the right reason is a discount, entered separately.

### A block that warns early

**The problem.** A note can't be issued against an invoice made out to the generic public tax ID. Finding that out at stamping time means losing all the data entry.

**The decision.** The invoice stays in the list and can be selected, because it's the one the person is looking for; selecting it shows the warning and blocks continuing. The block arrives at the first step, not the last.

### Reviewing isn't stamping

**The problem.** Stamping is irreversible, and a button that says "confirm" doesn't make clear whether something was already issued.

**The decision.** I split the two actions. "Review and confirm" only opens a preview of the document as it will be issued; it doesn't use a folio number or issue anything. "Stamp credit note" is the only action that issues, and the screen warns beforehand that it's irreversible.

### An error that says what to do

**The problem.** Stamping can fail for two reasons with different ways out: the stamping service didn't respond, or the tax authority rejected the document.

**The decision.** The message says which one happened and what to check. If the service didn't respond, you can retry; if the data was rejected, it has to be fixed first. Sending by email comes after and separately: the note exists even if the email fails.

## How I worked

- **Canonical data.** Each scenario uses the data from the agreements document with the client, so the screens also work to validate the rules.
- **Three sizes without multiplying.** At 2560 there are all twelve scenarios. At 1920 and 1366 only the ones that change the design: line-item entry, manual entry and money refund. The rest only change data.
- **Documentation inside the screen.** Each rule is annotated next to the screen where it applies, with its requirement criterion.

## Outcome

It's in development, so there are no metrics yet. Development and QA received all twelve scenarios with each rule annotated on the screen where it applies, and the screen data matches the agreements document, so it doubles as test cases.

## What I learned

That a tax flow is designed by reading the rules before drawing screens. The twelve scenarios looked like twelve processes until I sorted them by which data changed in each one; from there, the design was a single one.

And that using the real data from the client agreement, instead of filler, makes the mockup work twice: as design and as validation of the rules.
