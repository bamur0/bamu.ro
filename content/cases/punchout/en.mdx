## Context

One of the distributor's corporate customers bought through a manual process: they sent a request, received a quote, sent back a purchase order, and all that back-and-forth was managed in the distributor's ERP. Each order took one or two days to be ready for processing.

The customer wanted something different: to buy from their own procurement system, without waiting on the distributor.

## The proposal

A PunchOut platform. From their procurement system, the buyer "punches out" to the distributor's catalog, where they only see the products and prices in their contract. There they build their order and send it back to their system, where internal approval continues. The order reaches the distributor's ERP already structured, ready to process with no inconsistencies. There's no checkout or payment: the session ends when the cart is transferred.

## My role

I led the project end to end in one month, as lead business and systems analyst and as product designer: requirements, flow definition, interface and state design, handoff documentation with per-component annotations, and final testing through release.

## The challenge

Design for someone who isn't the distributor's usual user: a corporate buyer in a hurry, inside a time-limited session, facing a catalog full of technical data (SKU, CAS number, pack size, compliance and lead time).

## The flow

Session check, privacy notice, plant selection, catalog, cart and transfer. Six steps, and in none of them does the buyer have to guess what comes next.

## Key decisions

### Plant comes before the catalog

One corporate customer has several plants, each with its own address and delivery type (partial or full). Choosing it on entry makes it clear from the start where the order is going, and that context stays visible at the top for the whole session, with the option to change it.

### Session time always in view

PunchOut sessions expire. Losing a built cart is the worst possible error in this product, so the remaining time lives in the top bar and, before it runs out, a notice offers to continue. Ending the session also asks for confirmation.

### The cart lives next to the catalog

The buyer isn't browsing: they're building an order. So the cart isn't another page but a fixed panel with subtotal, VAT and total that updates as products are added.

### Designed for real data, not pretty data

I tested the design with product names hundreds of characters long, twelve-digit prices and seven-digit quantities. In the catalog the name is truncated and readable in full on hover; in the cart it's shown whole, because that's where the buyer checks what they're about to order.

## Every state

I designed each state before handoff: skeleton loading, empty catalog results, empty cart, session error, cancel confirmation, expiring session and transfer in progress. Whatever isn't designed ends up improvised in code.

## Testing

We tested with users on both sides: the distributor's internal team and the customer's buyers. We ran smoke, usability and navigation tests, and reviewed heatmaps to see where attention landed.

Testing confirmed the flow: nobody got lost and navigation needed no changes. The fixes were visual, and the rest was technical, in how the cart transfers between systems. A new product reaching testing with almost no navigation changes is the payoff of defining the flow and states properly during analysis.

## Outcome

The platform is in production. Quotes and purchase orders now reach the ERP instantly, and time from quote to order dropped by about 80%. What remains is the distributor's internal order processing.

The customer now quotes and buys on their own, from their own system, without waiting on the distributor.
